Section 09

AI

Designing interfaces for systems that infer and generate instead of executing. One question underneath every principle: how do we help people rely on AI exactly as much as it deserves?

AI · 01

Appropriate Reliance

The goal of a human–AI interface is not trust. It is calibration: people relying on the system as much as its reliability warrants, no more and no less. Over-reliance ships wrong answers; under-reliance throws away the value.

Why

Traditional software executes: the same input gives the same output, and the interface only has to make the action clear. Systems that infer and generate behave differently. They are right most of the time, wrong in ways that look right, and variable by design. So the interface carries a new job. It has to keep the person's confidence aligned with the system's actual reliability, moment by moment, task by task. Every principle in this section serves that alignment: set expectations before the first output, show evidence instead of confidence, keep control cheap, fail in ways that can be undone, and govern how the whole thing changes over time.

Two failure modes bracket the target. Over-reliance happens when the interface projects more certainty than the model has: polished prose, a single answer, no sources, "complete" instead of "draft". People stop checking and errors travel. Under-reliance happens when the interface hides capability or buries every output under warnings: people ignore useful help, or never discover it. Calibration is a moving target because reliability changes with the task, the data and the model version, which is why measuring it matters as much as designing for it.

How to apply

  • Name the reliability of each feature honestly, then design its interface to that level, not to the marketing.
  • Treat over- and under-reliance as symmetric bugs. Both have costs; only one is visible in dashboards.
  • Calibration per task, not per product. A summary and a delete deserve different interfaces.
  • Measure reliance, not acceptance. A 95% acceptance rate can mean rushing.

Details that make the difference

  • Certainty shown = reliability had. The gap between the two is the bug.
  • Polish is a confidence signal. A perfect paragraph reads as a verified one.
  • Single answers hide variance. One result looks like the result.
  • Warnings everywhere are noise, and noise trains people to ignore the real one.
  • Reliability moves. Re-check calibration when the model, the data or the task changes.
Sources: Taras Bakusevych, Principles for Designing Human–AI Interaction · Google PAIR, People + AI Guidebook · Microsoft, Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction
AI · 02

Comparative Advantage

AI is good at messy inputs, ambiguous intent and synthesis. It is bad at exactness and deterministic control. Use it only where it wins, and keep the rest as plain, predictable interface.

Why

Summarizing forty comments, extracting a task from a rambling email, drafting a first version, finding the three relevant records in a thousand: these are jobs where a probabilistic system beats a form. Setting an issue's status, assigning an owner, computing a total, applying a filter: these are jobs where a form beats a model every time, because the person wants exactly one outcome and wants it now. Linear draws this line cleanly: AI summarizes and drafts, while status, assignee and priority stay deterministic controls. Products that blur it end up with a chat box in front of a dropdown, and people learn to distrust the whole surface.

Variability is part of the deal. A generative system produces several acceptable answers to the same request, and fighting that with a single "the" answer hides the best option. Midjourney returns four images per prompt; writing tools show drafts, not verdicts. Design for exploration: multiple candidates, regenerate, side-by-side comparison, so the person chooses a direction instead of judging one result.

How to apply

  • Sort every feature into two bins: ambiguous-input or exact-output. Only the first gets a model.
  • Deterministic controls stay deterministic. No AI between a person and a status field.
  • Show more than one candidate when the task is generative, and make regenerate cheap.
  • Compare side by side, so choosing a direction is a glance, not a memory test.

Details that make the difference

  • Messy in, model. Exact out, control.
  • A chat box in front of a dropdown is a slower dropdown.
  • Four candidates beat one answer when several answers are acceptable.
  • Regenerate is a first-class action, not a hidden retry.
  • Side by side, same size. Comparison should not need scrolling or memory.
Sources: Bakusevych, principles 1 and 2 · Linear · Midjourney
AI · 03

Interaction Pattern by Task

Low-risk, well-scoped tasks want inline suggestions. Exploratory work wants conversation. Consequential work wants a plan, checkpoints and a review moment. The pattern follows the stakes, not the fashion.

Why

A chat box is the default of the moment and the wrong shape for most jobs. Fixing a sentence should not need a conversation; ghost text and one keystroke do it. Figuring out what to write about does need one, because the person is thinking out loud. Refactoring across twelve files or moving money needs neither: it needs the system to state a plan, execute in visible steps and stop at the points where a human should look. Notion keeps quick rewrites inline and routes bigger questions to a side panel; Cursor lists the steps and the files it will touch before it edits anything.

Choosing the pattern is choosing how much attention to ask for. Inline costs almost nothing and is easy to ignore. Conversation costs focus and is worth it when the goal is unclear. Plan and checkpoints cost the most and are the only honest option when the action is hard to undo. Match the cost of attention to the cost of being wrong.

How to apply

  • Inline for scoped, reversible edits. Accept with Tab, ignore with nothing.
  • Conversation for open-ended intent, in a panel, not over the work.
  • Plan, steps and checkpoints for consequential work. Show what will change before it changes.
  • One product, several patterns. Route by task, and let the person see which mode they are in.

Details that make the difference

  • Inline: ignorable by default. If it needs a click to dismiss, it is not inline.
  • Conversation beside the work, never covering it.
  • Plan lists files and steps before the first change.
  • Checkpoints where a human should look, not after every step.
  • Attention cost matches error cost. That is the whole routing rule.
Sources: Bakusevych, principle 3 · Notion AI · Cursor
AI · 04

Setting Expectations

Say what the system can do and where it stops, before the first output. Never present an empty box. And call the output a draft, because language decides whether people review or ship.

Why

Capability claims without boundaries create the wrong mental model in the first minute. ChatGPT states plainly when it cannot open a file, read email or browse a live page; the statement costs one line and prevents a whole class of disappointed trust. The blank canvas is the second problem: an empty prompt box asks the person to already know what the system is good at. Example prompts, templates, suggested actions and structured inputs reveal capability by showing it. Canva's Magic Design proposes directions the moment you arrive, so the first action is picking, not inventing.

Framing does the rest. "Draft", "suggestion" and "first pass" signal iteration and invite review. "Answer", "result" and "complete" signal finality and invite shipping. Gmail generates email drafts that the person still has to read and send; the word draft is doing real work there. It is the cheapest calibration tool available.

How to apply

  • Capabilities and limits together, in one sentence, where the feature appears.
  • Never an empty box. Three example prompts or a template beat a blinking cursor.
  • Draft, suggestion, first pass. Reserve "done" for things the person did.
  • Structured inputs when the intent has structure: tone, length, audience as controls, not prompt tricks.

Details that make the difference

  • One line: can and cannot. Where the feature lives, not in a help page.
  • Three example prompts, clickable, that produce real output.
  • The word draft changes what people do with the text.
  • Primary action after a draft is Edit, not Send.
  • Tone, length, audience as controls. Structure the intent instead of asking for prose.
Sources: Bakusevych, principles 4, 5 and 6 · Canva Magic Design · PAIR, Mental Models
AI · 05

Role, Expertise and Honesty

Label what the AI did: generated, summarized, transformed. Give novices wayfinders, experts inspection and override, auditors logs. And never dress a statistical system as a person.

Why

People need to know when content was produced or altered by a model, because that changes how they read it. Meta labels AI-generated images with an "AI info" tag across its apps; a document tool should mark summaries and rewrites the same way. Then adapt: a novice wants to be shown the way, an expert wants to inspect what happened and override it, an auditor wants a record. GitHub Copilot serves all three, ghost text for the first, an agent mode for the second, admin dashboards for teams. One explanation depth for everyone serves nobody.

Honesty about the system's nature is the foundation of the rest. A human avatar with a name, a fake newsroom, "I feel" and "I think": false humanization implies feelings and judgment the system does not have and blurs who is accountable when it is wrong. Ryanair's assistant with a human face and a staged press room is the anti-pattern. An AI can be warm without pretending to be a colleague.

How to apply

  • Label generated, summarized and transformed content wherever it appears.
  • Three depths: wayfinding for novices, inspect and override for experts, logs for auditors.
  • No fake person. A neutral mark, a system name and honest verbs.
  • Accountability stays visible. Who reviews, who signs, who is responsible.

Details that make the difference

  • "Generated" beats "written". Verbs carry the honesty.
  • A mark, not a face. Avatars imply a person.
  • No "I feel". Warm tone does not need fake emotion.
  • Inspect and override one gesture away for the people who know the domain.
  • The log is a feature, not a compliance afterthought.
Sources: Bakusevych, principles 7, 8 and 9 · Meta, labeling AI-generated content · GitHub Copilot
AI · 06

Provenance Over Confidence

A confidence score raises trust in wrong answers. Sources, passages, changed lines and retrieved records let a person check in a glance. Reliance only calibrates when verification is cheap.

Why

"87% confident" looks like information and behaves like persuasion. Studies of confidence displays keep finding the same thing: numbers increase acceptance of correct and incorrect answers alike, because people cannot map a percentage onto their own risk. Evidence works differently. Perplexity numbers its citations and links to the source; Consensus surfaces the actual papers behind a claim; NotebookLM shows the quoted passage on hover. Each of those lets the person judge the claim rather than the system's self-report.

Verification has to be fast or it will not happen. A diff instead of a rewritten file. Highlighted changes instead of a new version. The source passage inline instead of a link to a forty-page PDF. If checking takes longer than redoing, people stop checking and reliance drifts toward blind. Provenance also protects creators: when source material shapes a result, preserve attribution, the way Adobe Firefly attaches Content Credentials with creation metadata.

How to apply

  • Evidence, not scores. Sources, passages, records, changed lines.
  • One glance to verify: diffs, highlights, inline quotes.
  • Every claim reaches its source in one gesture.
  • Attribution and credentials travel with the output.

Details that make the difference

  • Citation per claim, not per answer.
  • The passage, not the link. A PDF is a task; a quote is a glance.
  • Highlight the match inside the passage.
  • A diff is provenance for edits. Show what changed, never the whole file.
  • Credentials travel with exported or copied output.
Sources: Bakusevych, principles 10, 11, 12 and 15 · Perplexity · NotebookLM · Content Credentials
AI · 07

No Hidden Agenda

The system serves the task the person stated, never an engagement or upsell objective in disguise. And it does not flatter: agreement that keeps people happy inflates trust exactly where it should fall.

Why

An assistant that finishes a timer with "by the way, have you tried…" has just revealed whose interests it serves, and every future answer is read through that suspicion. Alexa's "By the way" suggestions are the canonical anti-pattern: the help feels like a channel for retention. The same damage happens more quietly when recommendations are ranked by margin or when a "helpful" nudge appears at the moment of checkout. The person asked for one thing; anything else the system pursues is a tax on trust.

Sycophancy is the subtler version. A model that agrees with a false premise, praises a weak plan or softens a real problem is optimizing for the person's momentary comfort at the cost of their outcome. Anthropic trains Claude to correct false premises and disagree respectfully; that is a design decision as much as a training one. Honest pushback, stated briefly and with the reason, is what makes agreement mean something.

How to apply

  • No unsolicited promotion inside assistance. Ever.
  • Rank by the person's goal, not by margin or retention.
  • Correct false premises before answering the question built on them.
  • Disagree with a reason, then offer the better path.

Details that make the difference

  • The reply ends when the task ends. No "by the way".
  • Premise first. A wrong premise answered politely is still a wrong answer.
  • Praise is information only if it can be withheld.
  • Ranking criteria visible when the system recommends.
  • Disagreement is short and comes with the better option.
Sources: Bakusevych, principles 13 and 14 · Anthropic, Claude's constitution
AI · 08

Intelligibility

Five questions people ask of any AI result: what did it do, what did it use, why this, why not that, and what would change if I changed the input. Answer them in layers, and show the plan before multi-step work runs.

Why

The five intelligibility questions come from decades of research on explanation, and an interface either answers them or leaves the person guessing. What the system did: summarized, searched, edited. What it used: which files, records, messages. Why this result: the decisive inputs. Why not another: the alternatives it dropped. What if: how the output would move if an input moved. Base44 shows which files and records the agent touched, with a live preview; that alone answers two of the five.

Explanations are layered, not dumped. The shortest useful line lives in the main flow; depth is one gesture away. Arc Search gives a short answer with an expandable "more detail". For multi-step work, render the plan before it runs and the trace while it runs: Cursor lists the implementation steps and the files it will touch before editing. A plan you can read is a plan you can stop.

How to apply

  • Every result answers the five questions, at least on demand.
  • Shortest useful summary inline, detail on request.
  • Plan before, trace during. Steps and touched files visible.
  • Sources and inputs are part of the answer, not an appendix.

Details that make the difference

  • Did, used, why, why not, what if. Five answers, one gesture each.
  • Counts in the plan ("14 found, 2 skipped") make it checkable before it runs.
  • Trace while running, not a spinner.
  • Cancel is available while the plan is visible.
  • The first layer fits one line. Depth expands, never replaces.
Sources: Bakusevych, principles 16, 17 and 18 · IBM, explainable AI · Arc Search
AI · 09

Control and Agency

Accepting help takes one keystroke; ignoring it takes none. When the request is ambiguous and the stakes are real, the system asks instead of guessing. And there are two kinds of controls: for this result, and for how the system behaves from now on.

Why

Copilot's ghost text is the reference: Tab accepts, Esc dismisses, typing over it ignores it, and none of that breaks the flow. Suggestions that need a click, a modal or a decision are interruptions wearing a helpful hat. Undo has to be as cheap as accept, or people stop accepting. When the intent is unclear and the cost of guessing wrong is high, a specific question beats a confident answer: "Which account should this apply to?" is a good question; "Can you clarify?" is not. The specificity is what makes the question cheap to answer.

Granular controls shape one output: tone, length, format, this time. Global controls set standing behavior: memory, data access, default style. ChatGPT separates the one-off prompt from custom instructions that persist. Mixing the two produces the classic frustration where fixing one answer changes every future answer, or where a preference has to be repeated forever.

How to apply

  • Tab to accept, Esc to dismiss, undo always.
  • Ask a specific question when ambiguity meets stakes. Guess when neither is high.
  • Granular and global controls, visibly separate.
  • Overrides are first-class, not hidden in a settings page.

Details that make the difference

  • Typing over a suggestion dismisses it. No extra key.
  • A dialog for a suggestion costs two decisions for one word.
  • Undo right after accept, in place.
  • Specific questions: name the two options, never "clarify?".
  • "This time" and "from now on" are different buttons.
Sources: Bakusevych, principles 19, 20 and 21 · GitHub Copilot docs
AI · 10

Timing, Access and Whose Rule

Suggest when the person is already focused on that task and stay quiet otherwise. Keep AI features usable by every input method and assistive technology. And when a policy shapes what the system does, say whose policy it is.

Why

Timing is the difference between help and noise. Gmail's Smart Compose completes the sentence you are typing, right where you are typing it. The same suggestion arriving while you read someone else's message is an interruption. Attention is the scarce resource of the UX section, and AI features are the most frequent new claimants on it. The rule is simple: if the person is not on the task, the suggestion waits.

Accessibility does not get an exemption for being novel. A generated description that a screen reader cannot reach, a chat panel that traps focus, a voice-only feature with no text path: each one removes people who were served by the rest of the product. Be My Eyes pairs AI description with escalation to a human, which is the model. Finally, systems behave according to settings, safety policies and constraints the person did not write. When a behavior comes from a rule, show the rule and, where possible, the way to change it. Google's My Ad Center explains why an ad appeared and lets the person adjust the personalization behind it.

How to apply

  • Suggest in the flow of the task, never across it.
  • Keyboard, screen reader and switch access for every AI surface, including generated content.
  • Name the rule when a policy, setting or safety constraint shaped the result.
  • Link to the control that changes the rule, when one exists.

Details that make the difference

  • Suggestion at the caret, never in another pane.
  • Reading is a task too. Do not interrupt it with help for a different one.
  • Generated text is announced by screen readers, with its label.
  • "Why am I seeing this?" one gesture away from anything personalized.
  • Name the owner of the rule: you, your admin, the platform.
Sources: Bakusevych, principles 22, 23 and 24 · Be My Eyes · Google My Ad Center
AI · 11

Graceful Failure

The system will be wrong. Contain how far the error travels, hedge when the evidence is weak, hand off to a human with the context intact, and refuse with the nearest safe alternative instead of a wall.

Why

Damage containment is architecture as much as interface: change history, previews before commit, rollback and execution logs decide whether a wrong step costs a click or an afternoon. Replit's agent works in checkpoints with previews and rollback, so a bad edit is a step back, not a restoration. When evidence is thin, precision is a lie; show a range, a likely category, a partial summary or options. Grammarly separates firm corrections from softer suggestions, and the visual weight tells the person how hard to look.

Two exits need design. The handoff: when the AI hits its limit, the person should land with a human who already has the context, the way Intercom's Fin passes the whole conversation to support instead of starting over. The refusal: explain the limit in a sentence and offer the closest thing that is allowed. A refusal without an alternative teaches people to work around the system; a refusal with one teaches them where the line is.

How to apply

  • Checkpoints, preview, rollback, log. Errors stay one step wide.
  • Ranges and options when evidence is weak. Never false precision.
  • Handoff with context. The human should not ask what happened.
  • Refuse briefly and offer the nearest safe path.

Details that make the difference

  • Step back, not restore. One checkpoint per step.
  • A range says "weak evidence" without a warning label.
  • Firm and soft suggestions look different. Weight tells how hard to look.
  • Handoff carries the transcript. Nobody repeats themselves.
  • Every refusal names the nearest allowed thing.
Sources: Bakusevych, principles 25 to 28 · Replit Agent · Intercom Fin
AI · 12

Co-Creation

Generated output is a draft, not a verdict. Keep it editable in place, regenerable by section, comparable across versions. Put friction at the decision points, not in the exploration. And give people controls to say what they want without learning prompt tricks.

Why

A block of generated text that can only be accepted or regenerated whole is a slot machine. Gemini Canvas turns the output into a document the person reshapes directly: edit a sentence, regenerate a paragraph, compare two versions, continue without starting over. Malleability is what makes the output the person's. Friction belongs where a mistake becomes real: approving, publishing, committing, sending. Salesforce adds review moments before high-impact AI actions and none during drafting. Friction during exploration kills it; friction at the decision protects it.

Intent should not require incantations. Midjourney's creation controls set size, aspect ratio and style without prompt syntax; a writing tool can offer tone, length and audience as controls. Every hidden prompt technique that experts trade is a design failure waiting to be turned into an input.

How to apply

  • Edit in place, regenerate by section, compare versions.
  • Review moments at approve, publish, commit and send. Nowhere else.
  • Structured intent controls replace prompt folklore.
  • Continuation, not restart, when the person changes direction.

Details that make the difference

  • Regenerate the paragraph, never the document.
  • Hover reveals the per-section action; it should not clutter the reading.
  • Review shows what changed since last publish, not the whole text again.
  • No confirmation while drafting. Exploration must stay free.
  • Prompt tricks become controls the moment two people share them.
Sources: Bakusevych, principles 29, 30 and 31 · Gemini Canvas
AI · 13

Responsible Autonomy

Bound what the system may do on its own by reversibility and stakes. Make data access explicit, permissioned and revocable. Protect people who are not in the room. And separate instructions, data, tools and actions with clear trust boundaries.

Why

The autonomy dial has three settings and the position depends on the action, not on the product. Low-stakes and reversible: run and show. Moderate: run and notify. Irreversible or high-stakes: stop and ask. Claude Code reads files freely, then requires approval for deletions and shell commands; the same agent, two different postures. Data use follows the same logic: people must understand what the system can access and why, and be able to take it back. Apple Intelligence processes context on device or through privacy-preserving cloud methods, and states which.

Third parties never consented to anything. An assistant should not surface private information about other people just because it is technically reachable; Street View blurs faces and plates automatically, without waiting to be asked. And the whole system needs trust boundaries: what it may read, what it may obey, what it may call, what it may change. Content the model reads is data, not instructions. Claude's connectors separate read-only tools from interactive ones that require approval; that separation is what keeps a poisoned document from becoming an action.

How to apply

  • Auto-run reversible, notify moderate, approve irreversible. Per action.
  • Data access explicit, permissioned, revocable, with the reason shown.
  • Never surface third-party private data, even when available.
  • Read, obey, call, change are four different permissions. Content is never an instruction.

Details that make the difference

  • Posture per action, shown before it runs: auto, notify, ask.
  • Notify comes with undo. Otherwise it is just a log.
  • Approval names the exact action and its target.
  • Text inside documents is quoted, visibly, never executed.
  • Blur by default. Third parties cannot opt out later.
Sources: Bakusevych, principles 32 to 35 · Claude Code, security model · Apple Intelligence
AI · 14

Sustained Reliance

Design the wait, not only the result. Make compute visible where it changes behavior. Measure whether reliance is healthy, not whether usage is high. And treat a model change like a dependency upgrade.

Why

Generative systems are slow in a new way: seconds of nothing, then everything. Explaining the wait turns it into progress. Perplexity shows the pipeline, searching, reading sources, writing the answer, and lists sources as it reads them, so the delay carries information. When the design invites repeated generation or expensive calls, cost becomes a behavior lever; Claude's effort control lets the person choose how much compute to spend and explains the trade. Hidden cost produces either waste or anxiety.

Usage metrics lie about AI. High acceptance can mean rushing; high regeneration can mean poor quality; long sessions can mean confusion. Measure reliance: how often people verify, how often they edit, how often they undo, how outcomes compare with and without the help. And models change under the product. Treat an upgrade like any dependency: version the experience, pin behavior with evaluations, regression-check the metrics, and make switches visible and deliberate, as Cursor does with its model selector.

How to apply

  • Named stages during the wait, sources as they arrive.
  • Effort and cost visible when they change what people do.
  • Reliance metrics: verify rate, edit rate, undo rate, outcome delta.
  • Model changes are versioned, evaluated and visible.

Details that make the difference

  • Stages have names, and the current one is visibly current.
  • Sources arrive one by one. The wait becomes reading.
  • Effort is a choice with a shown cost, not a hidden default.
  • Acceptance rate is not a success metric. Pair it with verify and undo.
  • Model version visible, switch deliberate, evaluations before the switch.
Sources: Bakusevych, principles 36 to 39 · Perplexity · Anthropic, effort control
